# Q3. THE "PRE-DECISION" CONCEPT

**Thesis under test:** good discretionary traders fix entry/exit criteria, invalidation levels and position size *before* or independently of the live moment, and live execution is largely rule-following against a pre-written plan.

**Verdict: strongly supported for the prescriptive literature and for institutional process; weakly supported as a description of what individual traders actually do.** The single most important finding in this brief is that the prescriptive norm ("predefine everything") is universal and well documented, while *measured adherence* to that norm is thin and, where it exists, unflattering. See §3.8.

## 3.1 Written trading plans — what the templates actually contain

### 3.1.1 The canonical first rule: predefine the loss

The clearest and oldest primary statement I found is Mark Douglas's *The Disciplined Trader* (1990), which I downloaded and read in full text. His "Trading Rule 1" is literally the pre-decision thesis:

> "**Trading Rule 1: Predefine what a loss is in every potential trade.** By 'predefine,' I mean determine what the market has to look like or do, to tell you that the trade no longer represents an opportunity, at least not an opportunity in the time frame in which you trade." [PRIMARY — named author, book text] — [The Disciplined Trader, full text PDF](https://ia802903.us.archive.org/21/items/thedisciplinedtraderdevelopingwinningattitudes/The%20Disciplined%20Trader-Developing%20Winning%20Attitudes_text.pdf)

Douglas follows immediately with Rule 2, which is the enforcement half of the pre-commitment:

> "**Trading Rule 2: Execute your losing trades immediately upon perception that they exist.** When losses are predefined and executed without hesitation, there is nothing to consider, weigh, or judge and consequently nothing to tempt yourself with." [PRIMARY] — [same source](https://ia802903.us.archive.org/21/items/thedisciplinedtraderdevelopingwinningattitudes/The%20Disciplined%20Trader-Developing%20Winning%20Attitudes_text.pdf)

Douglas is explicit that the reason to predefine is to remove in-the-moment discretion:

> "The market doesn't make you right, you make yourself right." [PRIMARY] — [same source](https://ia802903.us.archive.org/21/items/thedisciplinedtraderdevelopingwinningattitudes/The%20Disciplined%20Trader-Developing%20Winning%20Attitudes_text.pdf)

### 3.1.2 Douglas's explicit if-then contingency instruction

This is the closest thing in the trading canon to Gollwitzer's implementation intentions (see §3.2), written a decade before Gollwitzer's meta-analysis:

> "When you are about to enter into a position, ask yourself, by imagining, what the next five minutes or tomorrow (depending on your time frame) would have to look like to validate your trade, to confirm that the trend is still intact. **What would the next five minutes or tomorrow have to look like to indicate the opposite.** Then, again, place your orders at the appropriate price in advance of the market's getting there." [PRIMARY] — [same source](https://ia802903.us.archive.org/21/items/thedisciplinedtraderdevelopingwinningattitudes/The%20Disciplined%20Trader-Developing%20Winning%20Attitudes_text.pdf)

And in his summary checklist of the "objective" trader's mental state:

> "To stay objective anticipate as many possibilities as you can and how probable each of these possibilities are. **Then decide in advance what you are going to do in each situation.** If none of your scenarios is working out as you anticipated, then get out." [PRIMARY] — [same source](https://ia802903.us.archive.org/21/items/thedisciplinedtraderdevelopingwinningattitudes/The%20Disciplined%20Trader-Developing%20Winning%20Attitudes_text.pdf)

He also gives the mechanical version — resting orders placed before the market arrives — and states the psychological rationale:

> "By putting your orders in advance of some anticipated move, you will be learning how to let the market work for you. Placing your orders in advance will also help to keep you from having an opinion, and you won't be subjecting yourself to the moment-to-moment conflict inherent within all price movement." [PRIMARY] — [same source](https://ia802903.us.archive.org/21/items/thedisciplinedtraderdevelopingwinningattitudes/The%20Disciplined%20Trader-Developing%20Winning%20Attitudes_text.pdf)

**Caveat on honesty:** Douglas is a named author and this is his own text, so [PRIMARY], but the strength of the evidence is *prescriptive/normative*, not *empirical*. He asserts that successful traders do this; he does not measure it. I found no data in the book supporting the claim that traders who predefine actually outperform.

### 3.1.3 *Trading in the Zone* — the creed and the "five fundamental truths"

I could only reach *Trading in the Zone* through a reader-highlight aggregator, not the book itself. The two most-quoted passages are the "seven principles of consistent winning" and the "five fundamental truths":

> "I AM A CONSISTENT WINNER BECAUSE: 1. I objectively identify my edges. **2. I predefine the risk of every trade.** 3. I completely accept the risk or I am willing to let go of the trade…" [SECONDARY — quote aggregator reproducing the book's text] — [Screvi highlights page](https://screvi.com/highlights/trading-in-the-zone-master-the-market-with-confidence-discipline-and-a-winning-attitude)

> "A probabilistic mind-set pertaining to trading consists of five fundamental truths. 1. Anything can happen. 2. You don't need to know what is going to happen next in order to make money. 3. There is a random distribution between wins and losses for any given set of variables that define an edge. 4. An edge is nothing more than an indication of a higher probability of one thing happening over another. 5. **Every moment in the market is unique.**" [SECONDARY] — [Screvi](https://screvi.com/highlights/trading-in-the-zone-master-the-market-with-confidence-discipline-and-a-winning-attitude)

On the market being neutral/objective — the specific claim in the brief — the aggregator gives Douglas's phrasing as *objective perspective*, not literal "neutrality":

> "When you learn the trading skill of risk acceptance, the market will not be able to generate information that you define or interpret as painful… **It is just information, telling you what the possibilities are. This is called an objective perspective**—one that is not skewed or distorted by what you are afraid is going to happen or not happen." [SECONDARY] — [Screvi](https://screvi.com/highlights/trading-in-the-zone-master-the-market-with-confidence-discipline-and-a-winning-attitude)

And on unplanned trading as the definition of gambling:

> "I define **random trading** as poorly-planned trades or trades that are not planned at all. It is an unorganized approach that takes into consideration an unlimited set of market variables, which do not allow you to find out what works on a consistent basis and what does not." [SECONDARY] — [Screvi](https://screvi.com/highlights/trading-in-the-zone-master-the-market-with-confidence-discipline-and-a-winning-attitude)

**Honest flag:** the "market is neutral" formulation in the brief is a *paraphrase* widely used in trading-psychology content. Douglas's own wording is "objective perspective." I did not find the exact phrase "the market is neutral" in any primary Douglas text I could reach. Treat "the market is neutral" as **FOLKLORE-by-paraphrase** unless someone produces the page reference.

### 3.1.4 A real prop-firm plan template (FTMO)

FTMO, one of the largest retail prop firms, publishes its own plan structure. Its central claim is the pre-decision claim in one sentence:

> "An entry signal is just a small piece of the puzzle. A true trading plan is your complete business blueprint. **By eliminating in-the-moment decision-making, a bulletproof plan ensures you never react to the market with emotion. You simply execute a pre-written script.**" [PRIMARY — firm's own published methodology] — [FTMO: "Treat It Like a Business: How to Build Your Trading Plan"](https://ftmo.com/en/blog/treat-it-like-a-business-how-to-build-your-trading-plan/)

FTMO's "Four Pillars of a Bulletproof Plan" — note that three of the four are pre-decision artefacts, not analysis:

> "**The trading strategy rules:** What is your exact setup? **Define the precise market condition, the entry trigger, the stop loss placement, and the profit target. Leave zero room for guessing.** **The risk management:** What exact percentage of your account are you risking per trade? Is it 0.5% or 1%? **Never change this number based on how confident you feel.** **Emergency rules:** What happens if you take three losses in a row? A good plan dictates that you close the platform for the day to prevent revenge trading. What happens if you make a massive profit early in the session? Your plan might tell you to cut your risk in half for the next trade to protect your gains." [PRIMARY] — [FTMO](https://ftmo.com/en/blog/treat-it-like-a-business-how-to-build-your-trading-plan/)

The "pre-market checklist" element is also explicit:

> "Before you ever place a trade, you need to set the stage. Your checklist should include: Perform a mental check-in… If you are exhausted, sick, or stressed from personal issues, **your plan must force you to step away from the charts entirely**." [PRIMARY] — [FTMO](https://ftmo.com/en/blog/treat-it-like-a-business-how-to-build-your-trading-plan/)

**Note on incentive:** FTMO is a commercial counterparty whose revenue comes from challenge fees. Its process descriptions are genuine operational documents, but they are also marketing. [PRIMARY] for "this is what the firm tells traders to do"; not evidence that traders who do it succeed.

### 3.1.5 The prop-firm "Day 1 gate" checklist — pre-decision as account-survival

A prop firm (AIFO) publishes a formal Day-1 pre-trade gate. Its framing is that the *first order is an account-state decision*, not a market decision:

> "**A Day 1 prop firm checklist is the final gate before the first order.** Confirm the exact programme, rule version, loss limits, drawdown type, trading-day requirement, permitted tools, platform settings, risk budget and first-trade conditions. **If one required item is unclear, delay the trade.**" [PRIMARY — firm's own published rule guide] — [AIFO: Prop Firm Challenge Checklist Before Day 1](https://aifo.com/blog/guide/prop-firm-challenge-checklist-before-day-1/)

The gate is expressed as a pass/block table with an "Evidence" column — a genuinely checklist-shaped artefact:

> "| Gate | Pass condition | Block the trade when | Evidence |… | Risk budget | Risk per trade, daily stop and maximum trades are fixed | **Size will change after a win or loss** | Written session plan |" [PRIMARY] — [AIFO](https://aifo.com/blog/guide/prop-firm-challenge-checklist-before-day-1/)

And on sizing being derived from the loss boundary rather than from the opportunity:

> "**A profit target should not determine position size.** Size must fit the nearest loss boundary and the trader's planned stop." [PRIMARY] — [AIFO](https://aifo.com/blog/guide/prop-firm-challenge-checklist-before-day-1/)

And the journal requirement is itself pre-decision:

> "**Prepare the Journal Before Entry.** A challenge journal should record account rules as well as trade results: … planned stop, position size and maximum cash loss; process error and stop-trading decision; remaining account room after the trade. **P&L alone cannot show whether a winning trade used excessive risk or whether a losing trade followed the plan.**" [PRIMARY] — [AIFO](https://aifo.com/blog/guide/prop-firm-challenge-checklist-before-day-1/)

## 3.2 If-then contingency planning: the Gollwitzer implementation-intentions literature

This is the strongest *academic* support for the pre-decision concept, and it is real. I found the primary meta-analysis abstract in an institutional repository.

### 3.2.1 The primary source and its effect size

Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes." *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38, 69–119. DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1.

The repository abstract states the finding precisely:

> "This review analyzes whether realization of goal intentions is facilitated by forming an implementation intention that **spells out the when, where, and how of goal striving in advance** ('If situation Y is encountered, then I will initiate goal-directed behavior X!'). Findings from **94 independent tests** showed that implementation intentions had a positive effect of **medium-to-large magnitude (d = .65)** on goal attainment. Implementation intentions were effective in promoting the initiation of goal striving, **the shielding of ongoing goal pursuit from unwanted influences**, disengagement from failing courses of action, and conservation of capability for future goal striving. There was also strong support for postulated component processes: Implementation intention formation both **enhanced the accessibility of specified opportunities and automated respective goal-directed responses**." [PRIMARY — peer-reviewed meta-analysis, author's institutional repository] — [KOPS, University of Konstanz record + full-text PDF](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)

Three findings here map directly onto trading plan design:

1. **"Shielding of ongoing goal pursuit from unwanted influences"** — the meta-analytic analogue of "don't move your stop / don't revenge trade." The literature is about shielding a *pre-formed plan* from in-flight disruption.
2. **"Enhanced accessibility of specified opportunities"** — specifying the cue in advance makes the cue more perceptible. This is the mechanism behind "wait for your setup."
3. **"Automated respective goal-directed responses"** — the response becomes automatic once the cue fires, which is exactly Douglas's "nothing to consider, weigh, or judge."

### 3.2.2 The boundary condition that matters most for trading

The secondary encyclopaedia treatment is useful precisely because it states the failure modes the primary abstract omits:

> "The strength of **commitment** related to both the plan set and the goal is very important for the implementation intention to have an effect on people's behavior. **Without commitment, an implementation intention will hardly have any effect on goal-directed behavior.**" [SECONDARY] — [Wikipedia, "Implementation intention"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention)

> "As reported by Theodore A. Powers and colleagues, **implementation intentions seem to have a negative effect on the performance in people who score high on socially prescribed perfectionism.**" [SECONDARY] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention)

And on the intention–behaviour gap that the technique is designed to close:

> "Developing research suggests that 'the correlations between intentions and behavior are modest, in that **intentions account for only 20% to 30% of the variance in behavior**.'" [SECONDARY] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention)

The if-then form itself is stated as:

> "A goal intention may be phrased in the following way: 'I want to reach X!' Implementation intentions on the other hand are much more specific and seek to connect **a future critical situation (an opportunity for goal attainment) with a specific goal-directed behavior**, thereby leading to what could be called automatization in goal attainment." [SECONDARY] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention)

The "no exceptions" rule is also stated, and is the part most trading plans violate:

> "**The then-sentence is applied strictly whenever the if-clause is fulfilled, without any exception.** If the user has to deliberate on whether an exception is justified, it adds to his cognitive burden, and thus can be detrimental for attaining the goals." [SECONDARY] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention)

### 3.2.3 CRITICAL NEGATIVE FINDING: no peer-reviewed application of implementation intentions to trading

I searched specifically for implementation-intentions applied to trading. **I found no peer-reviewed study applying Gollwitzer's paradigm to trading decisions.** What exists is:

- A GitHub research note by an unrelated software project asserting that a "5-question decision chain should be made into a pre-order implementation intention rather than in-holdings explicit monitoring" — [github.com/noonett/trader_system](https://github.com/noonett/trader_system/blob/master/research/foundation_2026.md). [FOLKLORE — unsourced assertion in a personal repo]
- Trading-education pages recommending "if-then" phrasing, e.g. [Mentorist: "Plan every trade with if-then statements to erase second-guessing"](https://www.mentorist.app/library/how-to-day-trade-for-a-715/insight/5571/). [FOLKLORE — SEO/education content]

**The trade-specific "if-then" recommendation is therefore an *extrapolation* from a robust general psychology finding, not a validated trading intervention.** The extrapolation is plausible and the mechanism (cue accessibility + response automaticity) is well established — but anybody claiming "implementation intentions are proven to improve trading" is overreaching.

## 3.3 Pre-mortem (Gary Klein's prospective hindsight) and its use in investment committees

### 3.3.1 The primary source

Klein's original HBR piece is reachable and states the mechanism and the headline number:

> "**In a premortem, team members assume that the project they are planning has just failed**—as so many do—and then generate plausible [reasons]." [PRIMARY — named author, HBR] — [Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," HBR, September 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)

> "Projects fail at a spectacular rate. One reason is that **too many people are reluctant to speak up about their reservations during the all-important planning phase.** By making it safe for dissenters who are knowledgeable about the undertaking and worried about its weaknesses to speak up, you can improve a project's chances of success." [PRIMARY] — [HBR](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)

**Honest flag on the famous "30% more failure modes" number:** HBR's free page does not contain it. The figure traces to Mitchell, Russo & Pennington's "prospective hindsight" experiments (1989, *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*). A named practitioner source restates it as "roughly thirty percent" but without a citation I could reach:

> "Klein's work, together with research on prospective hindsight, indicated that this framing **increases the identification of plausible failure modes by roughly thirty percent** compared with conventional forward planning." [SECONDARY — named practitioner essay, uncited] — [Dr. Raphael Nagel, "Pre-Mortem in the Investment Committee"](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

I could not fetch Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989) directly. **The exact 30% figure should be cited to Klein's own writing or to Mitchell et al. 1989, not to blog restatements.** Flag as [SECONDARY — widely repeated, primary not verified by this research].

### 3.3.2 The investment-committee protocol — an actual, timed procedure

The Nagel essay is the most concrete investment-committee pre-mortem protocol I found. It is a named practitioner's own account, not a firm's published process, so I grade it [SECONDARY — named practitioner essay], but the operational detail is high:

> "The protocol I recommend… fits within sixty minutes and can be inserted between the final memo presentation and the vote. It has five stages, and each stage is timed, because without timing the exercise collapses into ordinary debate. **The first ten minutes are silent and individual.** Each member of the committee, including observers and junior analysts, writes down, privately, the story of how this investment has failed eighteen months from today. **Silence is not a formality. It prevents the anchoring effect by which the first voice sets the frame for every voice after it.**" [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

> "The following fifteen minutes are devoted to clustering and probability… **The sponsor of the deal is asked, explicitly, which of the material clusters the current memo does not adequately address. This question is the hinge of the entire exercise.**" [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

> "The final fifteen minutes produce three artefacts: a short list of diligence items that must be closed before signing, a list of covenants or structural protections that follow from the identified risks, and **a pre-agreed set of warning indicators that will trigger a review during the hold period.** The pre-mortem is then annexed to the file. **It becomes part of the governance record**, not a conversation that evaporates when the room empties." [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

The distinction the essay draws between pre-mortem and scenario planning is analytically useful and matches Klein:

> "**Scenario planning widens the aperture. The pre-mortem narrows and deepens it.**" [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

And the learning-loop argument, which is directly relevant to Q9:

> "A well-documented pre-mortem creates a record of what the committee considered, what it dismissed, and on what grounds. When a deal underperforms… **the record permits genuine learning rather than retrospective storytelling. Without such a record, post-mortems tend to degenerate into narratives in which either the world is blamed or a scapegoat is found.**" [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

### 3.3.3 Pre-mortem as a formal memo section at an actual VC fund

This is the best primary evidence I found that pre-mortem is embedded in a real investment process, not just recommended. Elena Mazhuha, partner at Flyer One Ventures, describes her fund's standard memo:

> "**Pre-parade vs. pre-mortem.** This part describes all the good things that can help a startup succeed and, separately, all the bad things that can kill it. We write the main thoughts and then prove them with numbers and facts. *Ask yourself, 'What could make this company successful?' and write it down. Then, ask yourself, 'What could kill this company?'*" [PRIMARY — named partner at a named fund, describing their own process] — [Flyer One Ventures, "I'm a VC and here's how I write my investment memos"](https://flyerone.vc/post/im-a-vc-and-heres-how-i-write-my-investment-memos)

The same page also documents the **enforcement** mechanism — unanimous consent, which converts a pre-decision artefact into a binding gate:

> "At my fund, every partner brings a memo to the investment committee. Each member reads it, we talk, and then decide whether to invest. **One vote against and the deal falls through.**" [PRIMARY] — [Flyer One Ventures](https://flyerone.vc/post/im-a-vc-and-heres-how-i-write-my-investment-memos)

> "The memo does most of the convincing, so it better be detailed but also concise (~3,000 words)." [PRIMARY] — [Flyer One Ventures](https://flyerone.vc/post/im-a-vc-and-heres-how-i-write-my-investment-memos)

## 3.4 Checklists: the Gawande lineage and trading/investing adaptations

### 3.4.1 Mohnish Pabrai's investing checklist — the strongest primary trading/investing checklist source

Morgan Housel's 2013 interview with Pabrai is a direct transcript of a named investor describing his own checklist. It is the single best primary artefact in this section.

Origin story, and the explicit Gawande/aviation lineage:

> "I happened at the time to read a book by Atul Gawande; actually, I read an article first in *The New Yorker*, which was on the checklist, where he applied it to the medical field, and then subsequent to that, I had conversations with Atul, and he actually made it into a book called *The Checklist Manifesto*… **the 'a-ha' moment that came about for me was that I looked at the way the FAA functioned**… what I said is that **the equivalent of a plane crash in investing is when someone has a permanent loss of capital**." [PRIMARY — direct interview with named practitioner] — [Morgan Housel / The Motley Fool, "Checklist Investing," 8 Jan 2013](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/01/08/checklist-investing-how-to-avoid-errors-and-learn.aspx)

Method — he built the checklist by reverse-engineering losses, not by theorising:

> "I said, why don't we look at the best investors in the world… and look at investments where they ended up with a permanent loss of capital, and then evaluate the reason why that loss happened… And what was stunning to me is that **in almost all cases where I could figure out the reason for the loss, it was very apparent before the investment was made**, number one. And the second is **the reason was very basic**." [PRIMARY] — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/01/08/checklist-investing-how-to-avoid-errors-and-learn.aspx)

Size and structure of the artefact:

> "We today have I think about **97 or 98 questions on the checklist**, and it doesn't take very long to go through it. I think when I look at a business and I run the checklist, **it might take 15, 20 minutes to run through it**. And they do fall into broad categories… a set of questions which relate to **leverage**… another set which relates to **moats**… another set of questions which relate to things like **unions and labor relations**. There's another whole set of questions on **management and ownership**. Just all kinds of nuances of whether they own stock, do they act like owners… and then there are a few miscellaneous ones." [PRIMARY] — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/01/08/checklist-investing-how-to-avoid-errors-and-learn.aspx)

The claimed outcome (self-reported, unaudited — flag accordingly):

> "Since I put the checklist in place, in 2008 till today, we have made I think more than 30 different investments in the last five years or so… and **we only lost money on two investments**. And the total amount of money we lost from those two investments is less than 5 or 6 million dollars. And I think **a large part of that is the checklist significantly brought down the error rate**." [PRIMARY — but self-reported, no independent verification, no control] — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/01/08/checklist-investing-how-to-avoid-errors-and-learn.aspx)

Gawande's own mention of Pabrai is quoted in the same piece:

> "He [Pabrai] did very well … but not always, he found. He also made mistakes, some of them disastrous. These were not mistakes merely in the sense that he lost money on his bets… **No, these were mistakes in the sense that he had miscalculated the risks involved, made errors of analysis.**" [PRIMARY — Gawande, quoted in the Housel interview] — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/01/08/checklist-investing-how-to-avoid-errors-and-learn.aspx)

**This is the key conceptual move for the whole brief:** the checklist is a defence against *decision error*, not against *loss*. A losing trade that followed the checklist is not a mistake. That is the same distinction as Annie Duke's "resulting" (§3.5) and it is what makes the journal/review loop in Q9 possible.

### 3.4.2 Pre-trade checklists in trading — real but overwhelmingly SEO-driven

The genre exists in enormous volume and is almost entirely content marketing. Representative example, with the aviation/surgery lineage invoked explicitly and — notably — with one real academic citation attached:

> "Every professional pilot follows a checklist before takeoff. Surgeons use one before every operation. Professional traders should do the same before every trade… **A pre-trade checklist creates a pause between emotion and action.**" [FOLKLORE — commercial education content with product funnel; the page is a lead-gen asset for a paid programme] — [FinTorro, "The Perfect Trading Checklist Before Every Trade"](https://www.blog.fintorro.com/pre-trade-checklist-2/)

Its proposed checklist sections are, however, a useful synthesis of the genre: Market Conditions → Strategy Confirmation → Risk Management → Risk-to-Reward Review → Prop Firm Rule Check → Emotional Check → Focus Check → Exit Planning → Confidence in the Process. Two items are worth quoting because they encode pre-decision correctly:

> "**Professional traders define risk before reward.**" [FOLKLORE] — [FinTorro](https://www.blog.fintorro.com/pre-trade-checklist-2/)

> "**Never enter without knowing how you'll exit.** … Professional traders plan exits before entering—not during the trade." [FOLKLORE] — [FinTorro](https://www.blog.fintorro.com/pre-trade-checklist-2/)

> "Before clicking 'Buy' or 'Sell,' ask one final question: 'If this trade loses exactly as planned, will I still be satisfied that I followed my process?'" [FOLKLORE] — [FinTorro](https://www.blog.fintorro.com/pre-trade-checklist-2/)

**Honest flag:** this page cites exactly one academic source — a surgical-safety-checklist paper (DOI 10.1111/jep.13778) — and attaches it with a disclaimer ("it should inform a process, not be treated as a promise of trading results"). That is more honest than most. But **the "checklists in aviation/surgery applied to trading desks" claim, as a validated transfer, has no evidentiary basis I could find.** The chain is: Gawande documented checklist effects in surgery → Pabrai (a named investor) reports adopting the idea → an industry of trading educators asserts traders should do the same. **The middle link is one person's self-report. The last link is marketing.**

**Also unverified:** the widely repeated claim that the WHO surgical safety checklist cut deaths/complications by ~47% (Haynes et al., NEJM 2009). I did not fetch that paper; do not cite the number from this brief.

### 3.4.3 The strongest *trading-desk* checklist evidence: SMB's PlayBook Checkup

This is not a pre-trade checklist, but it is a real, named prop desk operating a recurring structured review with a fixed template — see §9.2.3. It is the best primary evidence that desks institutionalise pre-decision artefacts.

## 3.5 Decision journals, decision logs, and "resulting"

### 3.5.1 Farnam Street's decision journal — an actual published template

FS publishes its own template with eight mandatory fields. This is a primary artefact from the organisation that popularised the practice:

> "Whenever you're making a consequential decision, either individually or as part of a group, you take a moment and write down: 1. The situation or context 2. The problem statement or frame 3. The variables that govern the situation 4. The complications or complexity as you see it 5. **Alternatives that were seriously considered and why they were not chosen** 6. A paragraph explaining the range of outcomes 7. **A paragraph explaining what you expect to happen and the reasoning and actual probabilities you assign to each projected outcome** 8. **The time of day you're making the decision and how you feel physically and mentally** (If you're tired, for example, write it down.)" [PRIMARY — the publishing organisation's own template] — [Farnam Street, "Decision Journal: Template and Example Included"](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)

The optional fields they list are, in effect, a pre-mortem and a position-sizing prompt bolted onto the journal:

> "What's the primary thesis · What is the expected outcome(s) · What are the second and third-order consequences · **What is the worst-case scenario and why that's ok** · What is the potential upside beyond the core thesis · **What emotions am I experiencing** · What is the opportunity cost (by doing this what am I not doing) · What unique advantages or insights do I have in this situation · Who is the best person to make this decision · What does this look like in 5 weeks, 5 months, 5 years?" [PRIMARY] — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)

The rationale is a hindsight-bias argument, and it is the same argument AARs and trade journals rely on:

> "One of the strangest things about being human is that **we can't trust our own memories of what we were thinking. Not because we forget but rather because our brains actively edit the past to make us look better and smarter.**" [PRIMARY] — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)

> "A decision journal is really a mirror – one that shows you your thinking before your brain has a chance to polish it. **The key is looking in this mirror before you act, not after.**" [PRIMARY] — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)

> "**Two common ways people wiggle out of their own decisions are hindsight bias and jargon.**… When we use vague terms, we give ourselves wiggle room. If we want to get better at making decisions we can't give ourselves wiggle room. **Be clear. Be direct. Be simple. An 8-year-old should understand what decision you're making and why.**" [PRIMARY] — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)

And the organisational-learning claim, which is the Q9 thesis stated by a non-trading source:

> "Organizations don't really help. They treat decision-making ability as if it were fixed, like height… **they don't actually try to help people make better decisions. Instead, they try to identify people who already make good ones.**" [PRIMARY — opinion, not research] — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)

**Flag:** FS is a well-known publisher but this is an essay, not research. The template is [PRIMARY] as an artefact; the claims about organisational behaviour are unsupported assertions.

### 3.5.2 Annie Duke, "resulting," and outcome-vs-decision quality

I could **not** fetch a primary Duke source. wisdomtree.com (403 Cloudflare), investorspodcast.com (returned empty), and bookmate (403) all failed. I therefore have **no verified primary citation for Duke's "resulting" concept from this research.**

[PRIMARY for Pabrai/Gawande framing] — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/01/08/checklist-investing-how-to-avoid-errors-and-learn.aspx)

### 3.5.3 Tetlock, the Good Judgment Project, and the IARPA tournament

I fetched the APS *Observer* report on the primary *Psychological Science* paper (Mellers, Ungar, Baron, Ramos, Gurcay, Fincher, Scott, Moore, Atanasov, Swift, Murray, Stone & Tetlock, 2014, "Psychological Strategies for Winning a Geopolitical Forecasting Tournament," 25(5), 1106–1115, DOI 10.1177/0956797614524255).

Design and headline findings:

> "The first year began with **2,246 participants**, who were randomly assigned to one of the conditions that reflected different types of training (**no training, scenario training, or probability training**) and different types of group influence (independent, crowd-belief, team, or prediction-market forecasting)." [SECONDARY — APS reporting the primary study] — [APS Observer, "Predicting Uncertain Events on a Global Scale," 19 May 2014](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/predicting-uncertain-events-on-a-global-scale.html)

> "The results showed that **probability training was more effective than scenario training, which, in turn, was more effective than receiving no training at all.**" [SECONDARY] — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/predicting-uncertain-events-on-a-global-scale.html)

> "**Team forecasters were more accurate than crowd-belief forecasters, who were, in turn, more accurate than independent forecasters.** 'Greater accuracy in teams was due to members who gathered and shared information, encouraged one another, and discussed issues,' Mellers and colleagues explain." [SECONDARY] — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/predicting-uncertain-events-on-a-global-scale.html)

> "Intriguingly, **the best forecasters at the end of the first year were grouped together in 'elite teams'** for the second year of the tournament. These 'superforecasters' were far more accurate in their forecasts than the other groups in Year 2. '**Results strongly disconfirm the expectations of pro-independence theorists**,' the researchers note." [SECONDARY — note the selection effect: elite teams were *selected* on Year-1 performance] — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/predicting-uncertain-events-on-a-global-scale.html)

And on the two components of improvement:

> "To improve geopolitical forecasts, **one needs insights from both statistics and psychology**." [SECONDARY] — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/predicting-uncertain-events-on-a-global-scale.html)

**Relevance to trading:** the transferable lessons are (a) specific probability training beats general scenario thinking, (b) structured teams beat lone wolves, (c) frequent updating with scored feedback is the mechanism. The APS piece does **not** describe a decision journal. The link from GJP to decision journals is made by Duke and by FS, not by the GJP researchers. **Do not attribute "decision journal" to Tetlock.**

## 3.6 How professional desks ENFORCE pre-commitment

### 3.6.1 Investment-committee approval — documented in a regulatory filing

The strongest primary evidence of enforced pre-commitment I found is an SEC-registered fund prospectus (Grosvenor Registered Multi-Strategy Fund (TI 2), LLC, Form 497, filed 2019). It documents a multi-gate process with named decision-makers and an explicit veto structure. I downloaded the filing and extracted the text.

> "The Investment Committee **must approve** a particular Investment Manager and particular Investment Funds managed by such Investment Manager from an 'investment' and 'risk' perspective **before** GCMLP may invest the assets of the Master Fund in such Investment Funds." [PRIMARY — SEC filing] — [SEC EDGAR, Grosvenor Registered Multi-Strategy Fund (TI 2), LLC, Form 497](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

> "In connection with approving particular Investment Managers and Investment Funds and from time to time thereafter, **the Investment Committee determines guidelines specifying the target percentages of assets (usually determined as a range)** that the Portfolio Management Teams generally follow in proposing… initial and subsequent portfolio allocations… ('Target Allocations')." [PRIMARY — SEC filing] — [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

The committee's pre-decision criteria are enumerated — note item (v), which is a pre-mortem question in all but name:

> "In determining whether to approve a particular investment strategy, the Investment Committee examines and evaluates various factors such as: (i) the historical returns of the strategy in various market environments; (ii) the fundamental factors driving the expected profitability of the strategy; (iii) the expected returns from the strategy over a market cycle (generally three to five years); (iv) the expected variability of the strategy's returns over a market cycle; **(v) the magnitude of a potential loss in extremely adverse markets**; (vi) the extent to which the performance of the strategy is expected to correlate with that of traditional market indices…" [PRIMARY — SEC filing] — [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

Governance mechanics — majority vote plus a one-way veto:

> "The Investment Committee currently has four members: Mr. David S. Richter (Chair), Mr. David B. Small, Mr. Bradley H. Meyers and Mr. Frederick Pollock. **The Investment Committee makes decisions by majority vote**; however, Mr. Michael J. Sacks, GCMLP's Chairman and Chief Executive Officer, **has the authority to determine the outcome in the event of a tie and the authority to veto any affirmative decision** made by the Investment Committee. **If, however, the Investment Committee rejects or terminates an Investment Manager or Investment Fund, Mr. Sacks is not authorized to override the rejection or termination.**" [PRIMARY — SEC filing] — [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

**Why this matters:** the veto is asymmetric — you can block a yes, you cannot force a yes. That is a structural commitment device against escalation-of-commitment, and it is written into a regulated document.

Post-decision enforcement also exists (the "watch list"), which is the mirror image of a pre-mortem's "warning indicators":

> "The Investment Committee… may decide to terminate an existing Investment Manager based on any number of factors, such as: (1) disappointing performance relative to the Investment Committee's expectations…; **(2) indicators of excessive risk taking (such as exposure, concentration, or performance data) that are inconsistent with the Investment Committee's expectations; (3) deviation from stated investment strategy**; (4) deviation from [risk parameters]…" [PRIMARY — SEC filing] — [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

> "No additional capital of the Master Fund may be allocated to an Investment Manager/Investment Fund once the Investment Manager/Investment Fund is placed on the Investment Committee's 'watch list.'" [PRIMARY — SEC filing] — [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

**Caveat:** this is a fund-of-hedge-funds allocating to *external managers*, not a discretionary trading desk. The pre-decision artefact is an approval + target-allocation range, not a per-trade plan. It is still the best *enforced* pre-commitment structure I could document from a primary source.

- **dual sign-off** on trades
- compliance-mandated **pre-trade written rationale**
- **"no trade without a plan"** desk rules

**I could not find a single primary, publicly available document establishing any of these as a standard practice at discretionary trading desks.**

What I did find:

This is the closest thing to a standardised "reason code" in trading — and it is about *system rejection of orders*, not *trader rationale for orders*. [PRIMARY — protocol spec, referenced from a design doc I did fetch] — [APEX Protocol error taxonomy design doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/APEX-Standard/protocol/refs/heads/main/docs/error-taxonomy-design.md)

Flag: **[FOLKLORE — plausible, no public primary source found]**.

## 3.7 Evidence on whether written rules actually get followed

This is where the honest answer is uncomfortable.

### 3.7.1 Steenbarger's three reasons plans fail — and the first one is devastating

Brett Steenbarger (Ph.D., performance coach to portfolio managers and traders at financial organisations; author of *The Psychology of Trading*, *Enhancing Trader Performance*, *The Daily Trading Coach*) wrote a post specifically on plan non-adherence. It is a named practitioner's clinical observation, not a study — [PRIMARY — named practitioner, but observational].

> "A reader recently asked for perspectives on the issue of difficulty following trading plans. **If only we could faithfully follow our plans, the logic goes, we would be positioned for success. Maybe.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed, "Three Top Reasons Why Traders Fail to Trade Their Plans," 13 Sep 2014](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/three-top-reasons-why-traders-fail-to.html)

> "**1) The plans are not worth acting upon.** This is a very common reason, but no one seems to talk about it. **I believe people frequently veer from their plans because of intuitive wisdom.** They sense that markets have changed and their plans are no longer relevant… The presumption in trading psychology discussions is that one should reliably follow one's plans. **My leaning is to question the premise. Plans are only worth following if they are well crafted and reflect approaches to markets that have a demonstrable edge. If you don't stress test your plans, markets will stress test you.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/three-top-reasons-why-traders-fail-to.html)

> "**2) We are intellectually prepared with plans, but not emotionally prepared to act upon them.**… When we plan in one state of mind (calm, focused), but execute our plans in a state of flight/fight (aroused, impulsive), we are much more prone to cognitive biases and reactive behavior. **This is particularly the case when our plans call for one level of risk management, but emotionally we can tolerate only a lower level.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/three-top-reasons-why-traders-fail-to.html)

> "**3) Distractions interfere with our follow-through on plans.**… A common example of distraction is **staring at screens, following markets tick by tick, and then acting on a very short-term market movement that had nothing whatsoever to do with one's original plan.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/three-top-reasons-why-traders-fail-to.html)

The prescribed remedy is directly the Q9 learning loop:

> "**Keeping a detailed trading journal can be very helpful in identifying when you do and don't follow through on plans, revealing patterns in your own trading psychology.** In general, my advice is to **first make sure your plans are worth following before you worry about finding psychological methods for improving your discipline.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/three-top-reasons-why-traders-fail-to.html)

Steenbarger also offers the counter-intuitive point that visualisation/exposure is what makes plans executable:

> "One of the great benefits of visualization and exposure methods is that they allow us to **emotionally prepare** for stressful events… **it is easier to follow our plans if we have already faced likely challenges to those plans.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/three-top-reasons-why-traders-fail-to.html)

### 3.7.2 Prop-firm rule-violation data — real numbers, weak provenance

Two datasets circulate. Both are second-hand in the sources I reached.

**Dataset A — FPFX Tech, 300,000+ accounts (2024):**

> "In a 2024 study by FPFX Tech covering more than 300,000 accounts (reported via Finance Magnates), **just 7% of traders ever reached a payout, and only about 14% cleared a challenge in the first place.**" [SECONDARY — trade-press reporting of a vendor study, itself reported third-hand] — [Bitget News / TimesTabloid, "Most Funded Prop Traders Never Get Paid," 21 Jul 2026](https://www.bgportable.com/news/detail/12560605525823)

**Dataset B — hoc-trade, 500,000 traders:**

> "A separate 500,000-trader analysis by hoc-trade found that **roughly 70% of failures came from hitting loss limits, not from missing profit targets.**" [SECONDARY — vendor analysis reported third-hand] — [Bitget News](https://www.bgportable.com/news/detail/12560605525823)

**Dataset C — Prop-firms.com via press release:**

> "Data from leading firms indicates that **only 5% of traders successfully pass prop firm challenges, with even fewer—around 2%—achieving consistent payouts.**… the average spend per challenge is estimated to be over $4,200." [FOLKLORE — undated press release on a broker's investor-relations newsfeed, no methodology, no primary citation] — [Wedbush Investor Center, "Prop Trading Statistics 2024," 17 Oct 2024](https://investor.wedbush.com/wedbush/article/marketersmedia-2024-10-17-prop-trading-statistics-2024-key-insights-for-industry-professionals)

**How to read these:** the 7%/14% and 5%/2% figures are directionally consistent with each other and with the crude base rate of retail trading failure, but **I could not reach any of these primary datasets.** The "70% of failures come from loss limits" figure is the one that speaks most directly to "rules written in advance get violated," and it is the weakest-sourced of the three. **Do not put these numbers in a brief without a primary citation.** Flag: [SECONDARY at best, FOLKLORE for Dataset C].

### 3.7.3 The structural problem: prop-firm rules as *the firm's* pre-commitment, not the trader's

A detail from the Velotrade transparency report material is worth extracting because it shows that pre-commitment enforcement at prop firms operates on *account survival*, not on *trade quality*:

> "A max-risk-per-trade rule caps how much any single position or trade idea may lose **at any moment, measured on unrealized, floating, profit and loss, not on closed trades.**… **If an open trade's paper loss so much as touches the cap intraday, even for a second, the rule can trigger and the account is finished, even if that trade would have gone on to close in profit.**" [SECONDARY — trade press reporting a vendor's report; the vendor is also a prop firm, i.e. an interested party] — [Bitget News](https://www.bgportable.com/news/detail/12560605525823)

> "FundingPips applies a 'Risk Per Trade Idea' rule at the funded stage that **aggregates re-entries**. HyroTrader requires **a stop-loss within five minutes of every trade, monitored live.**" [SECONDARY] — [Bitget News](https://www.bgportable.com/news/detail/12560605525823)

The five-minute-stop rule is the hardest *externally enforced* pre-decision constraint I found anywhere: the trader cannot enter without a pre-placed invalidation level. **This is enforcement, not exhortation** — and it is the only mechanism in this entire brief where pre-decision is machine-checked.

## 9.1 What trading journals capture

### 9.1.1 A real desk template, with the if-then contingency built in

This is the single most valuable artefact I found for Q9 and Q3 together. SMB Capital (a Manhattan proprietary trading firm; Mike Bellafiore, author of *One Good Trade* and *The PlayBook*) publishes its internal "Template for One Good Trade" — a document traders must fill in and present to the desk.

The reasons the desk gives for the exercise are themselves an enforcement argument:

> "We ask that you prepare OGT for the rest of the desk periodically. We do so for the following reasons: 1) the only voice heard should not just be mine 2) traders on our desk can learn from you 3) **it is important for you to know how someone else trades so that your trading conversations with them are more meaningful** 4) this is the best way for Steve and me to get a feel for your trading and offer constructive thoughts to improve 5) **sharing your trades will improve your trading as you will have to defend each decision** 6) valuable screen time is gained by all who watch your OGT" [PRIMARY — named prop desk's own internal document] — [SMB Capital, "Template for One Good Trade"](https://www.smbtraining.com/Written_186)

The template's mandatory sections — note that four of the seven are pre-decision artefacts:

> "**Proper Preparation:** Reports Q2 (Apr) earnings of $0.44 per share… Levels: 33.50, 34.00, 34.50 Average Volume: 4.5mm Short % of float: 3.2% 52 week range: 26.15 - 40.99
> **Hard work:** Watched the prints and inside market and noticed the stock could not trade above 33.00. A big offer for 20k appeared on NYSE.
> **Patience:** Got long at 33.03 as soon as 33.00 lifted
> **Detailed plan:** Plan was to get long as soon as 33.00 lifted and stay long as long as the stock was going up (momentum trade). Sell when the stock slows. **(Please insert a plan for if the stock trades in your favor and a plan for if the stock trades against you. In this case the sale was when the stock slowed.)**
> **Discipline:** Executed on my plan as I sold at 33.14 as soon as the stock slowed.
> **Sharing your best trading ideas:** N/A
> **Review:** I had a long bias initially because ADI had positive earnings and guidance… **Looking back at the trade I should have recognized that ADI could not trade above 33.00 and shorted ADI when the large offer at 33.00 appeared.** That said, the actual trade I made when 33.00 lifted was executed well and I sold as soon as the momentum slowed." [PRIMARY — verbatim template, with a real filled-in example] — [SMB Capital](https://www.smbtraining.com/Written_186)

**This is the cleanest published instance of an if-then contingency requirement on a real trading desk**, and it is paired with a mandatory retrospective paragraph. The template also requires a *narrated video* walking through the trade — the desk's mechanism for making review costly to skip:

> "Open video and fast forward to the section where the OGT occurred… **Narrate the OGT enthusiastically and with conviction highlighting why it was a good trade and how it could have been better**" [PRIMARY] — [SMB Capital](https://www.smbtraining.com/Written_186)

### 9.1.2 What a professional actually tags and why — trade review in practice

SMB published a video transcript of one of its traders walking through the firm's post-trade tagging process. This is unusually granular primary evidence about what real desks capture.

> "so after you import your trades into trade review you're gonna come to the trades page… the first thing you want to do is put the date in for the trades that you imported that day… it's gonna allow you to **add some tags, add some notes and edit and put in a risk amount** so I usually for big winners big losers decent-sized winners decent-sized losers **I'll put some notes just so when I go back I do my weekly review and I review at the end of the month I know exactly what this trade was**" [PRIMARY — named prop firm, trader describing own process] — [SMB Capital, "How a Professional Stock Trader Reviews His Trading Results," 16 Dec 2019](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/how-a-professional-stock-trader-reviews-his-trading-results-be-professional)

**This is one of the few direct statements of review cadence I found: daily tagging → weekly review → end-of-month review.**

The classification scheme is two-tier — a broad category plus a specific sub-tag, with an explicit warning about tag proliferation:

> "I'd like to start with a **general tag to categorize the type of trade** so if it was a low flow type a low flow second day IPO changing fundamentals breaking news trade so in this case I tagged higher timeframe technical and now **I have hundreds if not thousands of this tag and it's just a general vague tag** this way when you look back at your trades you just have a few main categories that have a large tag count… then you could go ahead and **add some additional tags and I like to call these subcategories or subgenres** and here I get more specific about the intraday set up and **the important thing here is consistency**" [PRIMARY] — [SMB Capital](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/how-a-professional-stock-trader-reviews-his-trading-results-be-professional)

And the payoff — a finding that changed the trader's behaviour:

> "**I recently found out that one of my worst trades is a second day play and it has a large tag count** so it means I've taken it many times **sample set is very good and that tells me that well the sample set is very big and that tells me that I shouldn't be taking that trade anymore**" [PRIMARY] — [SMB Capital](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/how-a-professional-stock-trader-reviews-his-trading-results-be-professional)

> "I've been taking second day trades for about a year now and I've tried to work on them and I've tried all different things and I found out that through trying different trades and methods on the second day I just found out that **it's better for me to continue to track the stock** so normally we're trading second day plays after a stock gaps up or has news and **I've just been finding out for me that it's easier to watch it for a couple days because usually it needs time to accept the price it's at consolidate**" [PRIMARY] — [SMB Capital](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/how-a-professional-stock-trader-reviews-his-trading-results-be-professional)

Note that the disposition was not "stop trading this setup" but "narrow which of these I take" — the interviewer probes exactly this and the trader confirms. **This is a real example of data-driven setup elimination, not a template.**

### 9.1.3 A retail trader's journal architecture: live journal / feedback journal / emotional journal

A reader's question on the SMB blog, published with Bellafiore's reply, describes a three-journal structure that I have not seen bettered in any "journal template" content:

> "Every time I perform an action such as: Open a position / Scale into a position / Scale out of position / Close a position / Adjust a stop / Adjust a target — **I simply ask myself what are my reasons behind taking one of these actions.** These type of entries go into what I call the **live journal**, because they are entered immediately after a trade is placed and my reasons behind the action are fresh in my mind.
> After a trade is closed out completely I usually wait a few weeks before performing what I call the **feedback journal**. The reason for waiting a few weeks is to get a bigger picture of the trade and to observe how price behaves in the following weeks after my position was closed. For example, this will give me better clues on whether **I'm consistently taking profits or closing out a trade too early** and allows me to adjust my rules accordingly for the given trade setup and market environment.
> Other important aspects I add to my feedback journal is: **Analyzing the 10 best and 10 worst trades and trying to quantify the differences. Tagging my trades with error codes, to track and look for mistakes that are more common or prevalent.**
> I also have an **emotional journal** which is partly a Google spreadsheet form which I fill out to gauge my emotional and physical well being." [PRIMARY-ADJACENT — a named reader's own practice, published by a named desk; not desk policy] — [SMB Training, "The Chicken or the Egg?" 22 Oct 2012](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/the-chicken-and-the-egg-trading-dilemna)

The question this trader asks — and Bellafiore's answer — is one of the most important unresolved problems in the whole review literature:

> "I'm currently struggling in coming up with an objective way of grading my trades. I believe a trade must be graded based on execution. **But sometimes a trade is perfectly executed and it may still end up with a loss. So the question is, is it me? Are my rules flawed? Is it the market?**" [PRIMARY-ADJACENT] — [SMB Training](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/the-chicken-and-the-egg-trading-dilemna)

Bellafiore's reply is notably *not* a template. It is an argument that grading requires domain knowledge that beginners lack:

> "**New traders on our desk have an easy time of understanding an A trade from an F trade because they are taught which patterns are good and which ones aren't. They understand the risk/reward for each of their trades because they are taught the potential target and loss for each set up.** You seem not to be sure whether you are making a good trade or bad trade since you lack market experience. **The result of the trade is not enough. The risk/reward in the set up is the answer** to whether you are making as we say One Good Trade." [PRIMARY — named desk co-founder] — [SMB Training](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/the-chicken-and-the-egg-trading-dilemna)

**This is a significant finding.** Bellafiore's answer implies that trade-grading rubrics are *transmitted by a desk*, not derivable by an individual from their own journal. If true, it is a serious limit on the self-directed journaling loop: you cannot learn to grade your own decisions from outcome data alone, because outcomes are too noisy.

### 9.2.1 Daily → weekly → monthly: documented at SMB

> "'The junior trader **breaks down a favorite trade into five variables** for their presentation (the SMB PlayBook Template): **a. The Big Picture b. Intraday Fundamentals c. Technical Analysis d. Reading the Tape e. Intuition**'
> '**Each day our new traders archive a setup that made the most sense to them before they leave the trading floor.** From these PlayBook trades they choose one to present **under the pressure of constructive scrutiny** during the PlayBook Checkup. **They know to choose wisely and be ready to defend their trade.**'" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2015/03/best-practices-in-trading-creating.html)

The documented function list — note items 2 and 6, which are *selection* and *standard-setting*, not just feedback:

> "'Here's what the PlayBook Checkup helps traders accomplish: 1. Define their favorite setups **2. Eliminate setups not worth their intellectual, financial, and emotional capital** 3. Guide traders to internalize their trading edge with their PlayBook 4. Helps traders learn good risk/reward trades from their peers 5. Offers them a trade review by a trading partner with a deep dive into a specific trade setup **6. Provides a forum where standards of quality trades for our desk are set** 7. ## 9.3 Does review actually improve performance? The deliberate-practice evidence and its critics

### 9.3.1 Steenbarger's formulation of the loop — and it is not just "journal more"

This is the cleanest statement of the learning loop I found from a named practitioner:

> "Deliberate practice is a cycle of stepping back to observe and stepping forward to act. It's also a cycle in which **we first act in the world and then act upon our performance**. Analyzing, synthesizing, and doing, in markets and with ourselves: **that is what a trading process is all about.**" [PRIMARY — named practitioner] — [TraderFeed, "The Essence of a Trading Process," 30 Jul 2014](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/07/the-essence-of-trading-process.html)

> "**At the end of a trading day, week, or month, we repeat the process--only we turn the lens inward.** We analyze our performance, immersing ourselves in the data that tell us how well we executed and managed our trades; how well we discerned genuine opportunity in markets. We synthesize our performance observations into goals that move us forward… **Then we return to doing, feeding those goals forward** into future market analysis, synthesis, and doing." [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/07/the-essence-of-trading-process.html)

And the crucial operational definition — what distinguishes review from *deliberate practice*:

> "My immediate reply is that veering away from what makes you profitable—getting away from your best practices—**would not be practice**.… **you dissect your performance and identify precisely what you are doing well (so that you can replicate it) and what you could do better (so that you can improve upon it). The key here is turning your observations regarding strengths and weaknesses into specific plans and goals that become actionable in subsequent trading. That is what turns mere review into deliberate practice.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed, "How Can Competent Traders Become Great Traders?" 28 Sep 2014](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/how-can-competent-traders-become-great.html)

> "**Deliberate practice means doing, obtaining feedback on results, and then correcting performance.** If you're a competent trader wanting to become a great trader, you need to identify what you already do greatly and leverage that into more consistent success." [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/how-can-competent-traders-become-great.html)

Note the counter-intuitive corollary, which cuts against the "more screen time = more practice" folklore:

> "**To sum up, more trading is not necessarily more practice. Indeed, more trading can instill bad practices. And more time in front of screens is not necessarily more practice.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/how-can-competent-traders-become-great.html)

### 9.3.2 Steenbarger's method for finding yourself in the data

This is one of the most methodologically interesting things in the whole review literature — treating the trader's own P&L as a time series to be mined:

> "One of my favorite exercises with active discretionary traders is to **take their daily P/L numbers and treat them as a financial time series**. In essence, I'm developing a trading system, where the system is trading the trader!… **In some cases, I have helped build alerts for traders that pop up on the screen when they are at a point where they typically have lost money in their P/L cycles.** It's a different way of building mindfulness about our patterns, so that we can control them rather than have them control us." [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed, "Discovering Your Trading Psychology With Your Trading Metrics," 5 Sep 2014](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/discovering-your-trading-psychology.html)

Three recurring patterns he names:

> "**1) Losing money on one's largest risk-taking** — …Often, traders will take their largest positions after they've had a winning streak. Now they're playing with house money. **Confidence begets overconfidence.**… **Before you assume that you should bet larger when you have your greatest conviction, analyze your P/L as a function of your risk-taking. If your biggest bets don't have your greatest hit rate, is your confidence generating information?**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/discovering-your-trading-psychology.html)

> "**2) Losing money during quiet market periods** — …how does the trader perform during midday hours vs. early mornings and late afternoons? During quiet periods with narrow ranges, **does the trader make more trades or fewer?** It's not uncommon to see risk-prudent traders struggle when volatility rises and aggressive traders struggle when volatility is crushed." [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/discovering-your-trading-psychology.html)

> "**3) Losing money with good market views** — …What happens to traders' positions *after* they exit their trades—particularly after losing trades? **Very often, traders have a correct market view and still manage to lose money. By placing stops too close to entries and by adding to positions when moves have already extended, traders generate good ideas but execute and manage them poorly.**… Sometimes we see traders taking profits in trades that often ultimately extend far more in subsequent hours and days. **That occurs when ideas are generated on one time frame of analysis, but managed (for psychological reasons) on another, leaving plenty of money on the table.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/discovering-your-trading-psychology.html)

And the summary claim for the whole loop:

> "**Our trading results are a great way of studying ourselves.** If we are operating with cognitive biases and/or emotional disruptions, these will inevitably find expression in our P/L stream. **Because we are not always objective observers of ourselves, it helps to go to the objective data of our trading to find a mirror into our psyches.**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/discovering-your-trading-psychology.html)

**Flag:** this is a named practitioner with an unusual quantitative bent, describing his own coaching practice. It is [PRIMARY] but **not a controlled study.** There is no evidence in these posts that the intervention improved performance — only that it surfaced patterns.

### 9.3.3 Steenbarger's own diagnostic checklist for a track record

Directly relevant to "what does review actually examine." From a post written from the perspective of someone who recruited traders:

> "1) **How honest are you?** — You'd be surprised by the number of track records that are inaccurate… **A valid track record is independently verifiable.**… 2) **How correlated are you?** … 3) **How much do you make on a risk-adjusted basis?** … 4) **How well do you perform across market conditions?** … 5) **How much do you draw down?** — …**A good track record should demonstrate that you play good defense as well as offense.** 6) **Where do your returns come from?** … 7) **How well do you trade size?** — **Are your largest trades your most profitable ones, or do you tend to lose money when you're more aggressive?** 8) **Is your performance getting better or worse over time?** … 9) **Are there patterns to your performance?** — Look at periods where you have undergone personal stresses as well as positive periods in your life. **Do these correspond to trading performance?** … 10) **If someone else had your track record, would you give them your money to invest?**" [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed, "Ten Things Your Trading Track Record Says About You," 27 Sep 2014](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/09/ten-things-your-trading-track-record.html)

### 9.3.4 Ericsson's deliberate practice — the underlying literature, and where trading sits

Steenbarger's *Enhancing Trader Performance* (Wiley, 2006) is explicitly built on the expert-performance research tradition; his own blog bio lists *Enhancing Trader Performance* among his books and describes him as "drawing upon research from expert performers in various fields." [PRIMARY — author bio] — [TraderFeed author profile](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2014/07/the-essence-of-trading-process.html)

**I could not fetch a primary Ericsson paper.** What I *can* document is the critique, which is well-sourced and which any honest brief on this topic must include.

### 9.3.5 The counter-evidence: Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014)

The primary abstract is available in full:

> "More than 20 years ago, researchers proposed that individual differences in performance in such domains as music, sports, and games largely reflect individual differences in amount of deliberate practice, which was defined as engagement in structured activities created specifically to improve performance in a domain. This view is a frequent topic of popular-science writing—but is it supported by empirical evidence? To answer this question, we conducted a meta-analysis covering all major domains in which deliberate practice has been investigated. We found that **deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and less than 1% for professions. We conclude that deliberate practice is important, but not as important as has been argued.**" [PRIMARY — peer-reviewed meta-analysis abstract, *Psychological Science* 25(8), Aug 2014, DOI 10.1177/0956797614535810] — [APS, Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797614535810/)

**"Professions: <1%, not statistically significant."** Trading is a profession. This is the single most important number in this brief for anyone claiming that journalling/deliberate practice reliably produces trading skill.

For the full argument, I downloaded and text-extracted the open-access book containing the exchange between the camps (Hambrick, Macnamara, Campitelli, Ullén & Mosing, 2016, Purdue Skill Learning and Performance Lab). Key passages:

> "To be exact, as shown in Figure 1, the average proportion of reliable variance in performance explained was **34% for chess and 29.9% for music**. Thus, deliberate practice left more of the variance in performance unexplained than it explained. To be exact, on average, **deliberate practice explained 12% of the variance, leaving 88% unexplained**." [PRIMARY — academic book chapter] — [Purdue Skill Learning and Performance Lab, Hambrick et al. 2016 PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/Hambrick-Macnamara-Campitelli-Ullen-Mosing-2016-book.pdf)

> "[Effect sizes were] **strongest for games (26%), music (21%), and sports (18%), and much weaker for education (4%) and professions (<1%, and not statistically significant).** The effect sizes for education and professions may be smaller because delib[erate practice is harder to define or measure in those domains]…" [PRIMARY] — [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/Hambrick-Macnamara-Campitelli-Ullen-Mosing-2016-book.pdf)

Methodological detail that matters — retrospective self-report inflates the effect:

> "Indeed, **deliberate practice explained 20% of the variance in performance for studies that used a retrospective interview, compared to 12% for studies** [using other methods]…" [PRIMARY] — [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/Hambrick-Macnamara-Campitelli-Ullen-Mosing-2016-book.pdf)

And the concession from the other side of the debate, quoted in the same volume, showing that even Ericsson does not claim deliberate practice explains everything:

> "**I have never claimed that deliberate practice can explain all reliable variance in** [performance]…" [PRIMARY — Ericsson, quoted in the Hamburg et al. volume] — [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/Hambrick-Macnamara-Campitelli-Ullen-Mosing-2016-book.pdf)

**Bottom line for the brief:** the scholarly consensus is that deliberate practice matters but explains a minority of performance variance, and in *professions* specifically it explains almost none. Applying deliberate practice to trading is therefore a **reasonable hypothesis with a weak empirical base**, not an established finding. Any trading-education source that presents "10,000 hours / deliberate practice" as settled science for trading is overstating the evidence.

### 9.3.7 The intermediate position: trading is low-validity at the level of *outcomes*, high-validity at the level of *process*

This inference is mine, but it is well-supported by combining the sources above, and it is the practical resolution of the tension:

- Outcomes in trading are low-validity (§9.3.6) — a single trade result, or even a year of results, carries little information about decision quality.
- **But execution variables are high-validity.** "Did I place the stop where the plan said?" / "Did I size to the plan?" / "Did I enter before my trigger fired?" have *rapid, unambiguous, self-generated feedback* — you know the answer the moment you act.
- SMB's review process (§9.1.2, §9.2.2) reviews almost entirely execution variables and setup-category base rates, **not** whether the market view was right.
- Pabrai's checklist (§3.4.1) targets *errors of analysis that were apparent before the investment*, i.e. process errors.
- AAR-style post-mortems (§3.3.2) review *what the committee considered and dismissed*, not whether the deal worked.

**The defensible claim is therefore: journaling/review builds skill in the execution layer (high-validity, fast feedback), and does not build skill in the prediction layer (low-validity, slow noisy feedback).** Conflating the two is the most common error in trading-psychology writing.

## 9.4 Mistake taxonomies

### 9.4.1 The only rigorous academic taxonomy I found: FINANS (Leaver & Reader, 2016)

This is the strongest source in this section, and it is genuinely peer-reviewed.

Leaver, M., & Reader, T. W. (2016). "Human Factors in Financial Trading: An Analysis of Trading Incidents." *Human Factors*, 58(6), 814–832. DOI 10.1177/0018720816644872. [PRIMARY — peer-reviewed].

From the abstract, which I fetched in full:

> "**Objective:** This study tests the reliability of a system (FINANS) to collect and analyse incident reports in the financial trading domain, and is guided by a **human factors taxonomy used to describe error in the trading domain.**… **Method:** In study 1, 20 incidents are analysed by an expert user group against a referent standard to establish the reliability of FINANS. Study 2 analyses **750 incidents** using distribution, mean, pathway and associative analysis to describe the data.
> **Results:** **Kappa scores indicate that categories within FINANS can be reliably used to identify and extract data on human factors-related problems underlying trading incidents. Approximately 1% of trades (n=750) lead to an incident. Slip/lapse (61%), situation awareness (51%), and teamwork (40%) were found to be the most common problems underlying incidents. For the most serious incidents, problems in situation awareness and teamwork were most common.**
> **Conclusion:** We show that (i) **experts in the trading domain can reliably and accurately code human factors in incidents**, (ii) **1% of trades incur error** and (iii) **poor teamwork skills and situation awareness underpin the most critical incidents.**" [PRIMARY — peer-reviewed abstract] — [EconPapers/RePEc record for LSE Research Online 66307](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/ehllserod/66307.htm)

**What this gives the brief:**
1. A **validated** error taxonomy exists for trading, with inter-rater reliability established (kappa).
2. The dominant error class is **slip/lapse** (61%) — *execution* errors, not analysis errors. This is direct empirical support for the "pre-decision + execution discipline" framing.
3. The most *serious* incidents are driven by **situation awareness and teamwork**, not by individual analytical failure. That is an argument for the desk/checkup model (§9.2.2) over the lone-journal model.
4. **Base rate: ~1% of trades incur an incident.** Useful anchor — trading is not a high-incident-rate activity, which is precisely why learning from outcomes is hard.

**Caveat:** "incident" here means an *operational* incident (in the human-factors/aviation sense — an error that required reporting), not a losing trade. The distinction matters and should be preserved.

I could not access the full text (LSE Research Online returned 403; the SAGE page returned a Cloudflare challenge), so I cannot describe the taxonomy's specific category list beyond slip/lapse, situation awareness and teamwork.

### 9.4.2 Practitioner mistake taxonomies — representative, but all unvalidated

The category labels that circulate in trading education. Representative source:

> "**Mistake label** — Late entry, chase, oversize, ignored stop. | **Trigger** — Missed first move, chat excitement, previous loss. | **Context** — Trend day, chop, news reaction, low volume. | **Broken rule** — No retest, no defined stop, size exceeded plan. | **Next adjustment** — Wait for retest; reduce size after first loss." [FOLKLORE — affiliate-monetised SEO content; the page carries an affiliate disclosure and funnels to a paid community] — [Pro Trading Insights, "Mistake Tracking: What to Track Without Wasting Time"](https://protradinginsights.com/mistake-tracking-what-to-track-without-wasting-time/)

The conceptual content is nonetheless sound and matches the FINANS structure (error label + causal trigger + context + rule violated + corrective):

> "**Mistake tracking works because it turns vague regret into data.** 'I traded badly today' does not teach much. 'I entered three breakouts after the second candle extension and ignored my late-entry rule' is something you can correct." [FOLKLORE] — [Pro Trading Insights](https://protradinginsights.com/mistake-tracking-what-to-track-without-wasting-time/)

> "**Start with the most repeated mistake, not the most embarrassing one.** If late entries happened five times and oversizing happened once, the late-entry rule should come first." [FOLKLORE] — [Pro Trading Insights](https://protradinginsights.com/mistake-tracking-what-to-track-without-wasting-time/)

> "**Turn mistakes into rules**… 'No breakout entry after two extended candles unless price retests the level' is useful. 'Be more patient' is not specific enough." [FOLKLORE] — [Pro Trading Insights](https://protradinginsights.com/mistake-tracking-what-to-track-without-wasting-time/)

Also from the SMB reader's practice (§9.1.3): **"Tagging my trades with error codes, to track and look for mistakes that are more common or prevalent."** [PRIMARY-ADJACENT] — [SMB Training](https://www.smbtraining.com/blog/the-chicken-and-the-egg-trading-dilemna)

**Judge on this section:** there is **one** validated trading error taxonomy (FINANS) and it is academic and operational-incident-focused. The retail/practitioner taxonomies (late entry, chase, oversize, revenge trade, FOMO, moved stop, no plan) are **convergent and plausible but entirely unvalidated** — no reliability data, no predictive validity, no outcome studies. They are best treated as useful scaffolding, not evidence.

### 9.4.3 A machine-readable error taxonomy (for contrast)

The APEX Protocol document I fetched is a structured error taxonomy for *autonomous trading agents*, not human traders. Its seven categories — `auth`, `validation`, `risk`, `operational`, `broker`, `rate_limit`, `internal` — and its explicit mapping to FIX's `OrdRejReason` (tag 103) are useful only as a contrast: **machine trading has standardised, machine-checkable error classification; human discretionary trading does not.** [PRIMARY — protocol design doc, but for a different domain] — [APEX Protocol error taxonomy design](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/APEX-Standard/protocol/refs/heads/main/docs/error-taxonomy-design.md)

## 9.5 Post-mortems and after-action reviews in trading/investment firms

### 9.5.1 Documented: the review is *part of the artefact*, not a separate meeting

The SMB OGT template (§9.1) ends with a mandatory **Review** section written by the trader, and the whole document is then presented. The review is not optional and not private. [PRIMARY] — [SMB Capital](https://www.smbtraining.com/Written_186)

The PlayBook Checkup (§9.2.2) is the *live* after-action review: fixed slot, fixed template, peer audience, hosted by the co-founder, with "tough questions." [PRIMARY] — [TraderFeed](http://traderfeed.blogspot.com/2015/03/best-practices-in-trading-creating.html)

### 9.5.2 Documented: the pre-mortem's output becomes the post-mortem's benchmark

The strongest *structural* after-action mechanism I found is the Nagel protocol's insistence that the pre-mortem be archived:

> "**The pre-mortem is then annexed to the file. It becomes part of the governance record**, not a conversation that evaporates when the room empties." [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

> "When a deal underperforms, and committees always face some that do, **the record permits genuine learning rather than retrospective storytelling.**… With it, **the committee can compare the risks it foresaw with the risks that materialised, and can calibrate its future judgement accordingly.**" [SECONDARY] — [Nagel](https://www.raphaelnagel.com/pre-mortem-investment-committee)

**This is the missing link that makes pre-decision and review a single loop rather than two separate practices**: the pre-mortem produces the list of risks; the post-mortem scores the committee against *its own* list. Without the archived pre-mortem, the post-mortem degenerates into hindsight bias. This is exactly Farnam Street's "your brain actively edits the past" argument (§3.5.1), applied at committee level.

### 9.5.3 The SEC filing's "watch list" as a standing post-mortem trigger

The Grosvenor filing documents a mid-flight review mechanism with pre-agreed triggers and automatic consequences (§3.6.1): placement on the watch list *automatically* stops new capital allocation, pending review. [PRIMARY] — [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1475456/000119312519211670/d748558d497.htm)

### 9.5.4 What I could NOT find: Bridgewater-style "issues logs," formal AAR doctrine at trading firms

I searched for:
- Bridgewater's "issues log" / radical transparency / mistake logs — **no primary source reached.** There is a blog result ([michael-roberto.blogspot.com, "Hedge Fund Culture"](http://michael-roberto.blogspot.com/2009/03/hedge-fund-culture.html)) which is a personal blog. Flag: **[FOLKLORE — the "Bridgewater issues log" is widely cited in business writing but I found no primary Bridgewater document describing it.]**
- A formal US Army-style After Action Review doctrine transplanted into a trading firm — **none found.**

**Bottom line for §9.5:** after-action review in trading is documented at the level of *prop-desk trade review* (SMB, primary) and *investment-committee pre/post-mortem* (Nagel, secondary; SEC filing, primary for the watch-list mechanism). **Formal, named AAR programmes at hedge funds are, on the public evidence, unverified.**

This section is deliberately blunt. Several widely-repeated claims in this space have **no good source that I could reach**, and at least one has no good source at all as far as I can tell.

## Claims with NO traceable primary source

1. **"The market is neutral."** The exact phrase attributed to Mark Douglas. His actual wording is "objective perspective" — *"It is just information, telling you what the possibilities are."* ([Screvi highlights](https://screvi.com/highlights/trading-in-the-zone-master-the-market-with-confidence-discipline-and-a-winning-attitude)). **The "market is neutral" formulation is a paraphrase that has become folklore.** Do not quote it as Douglas.

2. **"Order tickets require a reason code."** I found **nothing** establishing this as a practice anywhere. FIX `OrdRejReason` (tag 103) is a *system* rejection reason, not a trader rationale field. Do not assert this.

# SYNTHESIS: the four claims that survive scrutiny

1. **Pre-decision is a genuine, universally-taught, and institutionally-enforced norm at the *process* level.** Written plans with pre-defined invalidation, pre-committed size, and if-then branches are documented from 1990 (Douglas) through real prop desks (SMB's OGT template requiring "a plan for if the stock trades in your favor and a plan for if the stock trades against you") to regulated committee structures (SEC filing requiring IC approval before investment; VC funds requiring unanimous partner consent). **[PRIMARY across sources]**

2. **The mechanism is supported by strong general psychology but has never been tested in trading.** Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 94 tests give d = .65, including the crucial "shielding of ongoing goal pursuit from unwanted influences." Nobody has run this in a trading context. **[PRIMARY academic, unvalidated extrapolation]**

3. **The learning loop is real as a *practice* and weak as *evidence*.** SMB's daily-tag → weekly-review → monthly-review cadence and the twice-weekly PlayBook Checkup are documented primary practice. But deliberate practice explains **<1% of performance variance in professions** (Macnamara et al. 2014), and I found **no empirical study showing journalling improves trading performance.** **[PRIMARY practice, absent evidence]**

4. **The resolution is a validity split.** Trading *outcomes* are a low-validity environment (Kahneman; "more like rolling dice than like playing poker"). Trading *execution* is a high-validity environment: fast, unambiguous, self-generated feedback on whether you followed your own rule. Every credible review process I found — SMB's tags and checkups, Pabrai's checklist, the pre-mortem's archived risk list, FINANS's slip/lapse dominance — targets **execution and process**, not prediction. **The defensible claim is that pre-decision and review build execution skill; they do not build forecasting skill, and any source that promises the latter is overreaching.**
